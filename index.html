Research

How did the first generation of stars build the elements they needed to burn efficiently?

The first stars, known as Population III, formed from almost pure hydrogen and helium. Without carbon, nitrogen and oxygen they could not run the CNO cycle, which is the hydrogen-burning process needed to hold a massive star up against its own gravity. The textbook way out is the triple-α process, but recent work suggests that α-induced reactions on light nuclei may offer another route to CNO seed nuclei at lower temperatures and densities.

One candidate chain runs through boron: 6Li(α,γ)10B followed by 10B + α. The route only matters if α-clustering near threshold (the effect behind carbon's Hoyle state) boosts the reaction rates, and the size of that boost at stellar energies is still unmeasured.

My PhD is part of the ERC Advanced Grant NUCLEAR (NUclear CLustering Effects in Astrophysical Reactions), led by Prof. Marialuisa Aliotta. I lead the measurements of 10B(α,n)13N and 10B(α,pγ)13C at the LUNA 400 kV accelerator, which sits under about 1,400 m of rock at the Laboratori Nazionali del Gran Sasso (LNGS). The mountain cuts cosmic-ray backgrounds by orders of magnitude, so we can reach the tiny cross sections at energies close to the Gamow window.

Highlights

The neutron detection, activation and prompt-γ work I lead, followed by the setups I helped build.

Neutron detectors

Direct neutron spectroscopy with EJ-301D

I set up direct neutron spectroscopy in the group from scratch, using deuterated liquid scintillators, so the activation results can be checked by a completely independent method. The work covers pulse-shape discrimination, detector response modelling, ambient-background measurements underground, and a full analysis pipeline with uncertainty estimates. The detectors are due to be used at LUNA.

Neutron detectors

Spectrum unfolding with MLEM & SMART

Getting a neutron energy spectrum back from scintillator light output is an ill-posed inverse problem. I developed unfolding codes based on Maximum-Likelihood Expectation-Maximisation (MLEM) and the Simultaneous Multiplicative Algebraic Reconstruction Technique (SMART), with further developments to the codes designed specifically to improve unfolding in background-dominated, low-count-rate environments like those in underground nuclear astrophysics.

BGO activation · 10B(α,n)13N

Counting 13N at very low rates

I built the framework that extracts 13N activity from time-resolved 511 keV coincidence data. An unbinned maximum-likelihood fit gives the starting point, and Bayesian MCMC sampling then gives the final yields and uncertainties. It includes a dynamic correction for target degradation as beam charge accumulates, and it is fast enough to interpret data almost in real time during a beam time.

Prompt γ-rays · 10B(α,pγ)13C

Prompt γ-ray spectroscopy in the same runs

While the beam is on, the BGO array also records prompt γ-rays from 10B(α,pγ)13C. I sum energy across the segments (add-back) to reconstruct the full γ cascades. Multi-parameter fits, constrained by the physics, separate the signal from structured natural and beam-induced background, again using a maximum-likelihood fit as the starting point for Bayesian MCMC.

Leadership & mentoring

Leading the (α,n) and (α,pγ) campaigns underground

I was the experimental lead for the 10B(α,n)13N and 10B(α,pγ)13C beam times at LUNA 400, acting as on-call coordinator for real-time strategy, troubleshooting and accelerator operation while running the analysis in parallel. I have also helped train and mentor three undergraduate summer students in data analysis and R-matrix techniques, as well as Master's students.

Target analysis

Characterising the 10B4C targets

I helped characterise the enriched thin-film targets on tantalum, using 11B(p,α) and 11B(p,γ) resonance scans at LUNA and Notre Dame, and FIB-SEM imaging of α-beam-induced blistering. I also helped model how the targets degrade under intense beams.

Silicon detector setup

The NUCLEAR charged-particle chamber

I helped develop the prototype silicon detector array for the (α,p) and (α,d) channels, and silicon detectors were one of my first core experimental skills. I worked on the geometry, protective foils, electron suppression and source calibrations at Edinburgh and the Laboratori Nazionali del Gran Sasso (LNGS).

Upcoming beam time

Next, we bridge the gap between the low-energy LUNA data and the existing literature.

Planned
2026–27

10B(α,n)13N cross section via activation at Felsenkeller

This is a planned two-week campaign, with dates still to be confirmed, at the Felsenkeller underground ion accelerator laboratory (HZDR, Dresden). It will cover the 400–600 keV beam-energy range, which has barely been explored: the lowest direct literature data stop at about 570 keV. We will irradiate enriched 10B4C targets with 10–30 μA 4He+ beams and detect the 13N decays in a segmented 4π BGO array, using analysis codes I developed for LUNA.
